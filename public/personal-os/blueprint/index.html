The idea

One question, one database

Am I living the life I intend to live?

Everything in /life exists to answer that question. Years of my life were spread across Notion pages, a travel app, a fitness band, Gmail, Google Photos and PDFs from the clinic. None of it talked to each other, so none of it could tell me whether this month was a good one.

So we pulled it all into one private Postgres database, put a small app on top behind a single login, and made the app run a loop: capture what happens, review the month, reflect, set the next goals. Once the data lived in one place, the blog became the obvious next tenant. My journal is where my ideas start, so the posts now live in the same database and get drafted from it.

Records

Things that happened, with a date and how sure we are of it (day, month, year or approximate). A move, a job, a surgery, a trip.

Measurements

A number on a day. Steps, recovery, weight, nights away. One row per metric per day, so a re-send overwrites instead of duplicating.

Entries

Writing. Journal days, forum updates, learnings, presentations. Full-text searchable, which is what the blog assistant mines.

The trick that keeps it cheap to grow: areas, metrics and sources are rows, not code. A new metric or a new source is an insert, not a schema change. Areas with a dark border are the ones I use daily today; the rest have tables waiting.

The schema

Every table in the database

The blog back end

The journal is the source, the blog is a view

Posts used to be hardcoded in the site and needed a deploy to change. Now they're rows in the same database, managed from a Marketing section inside /life. The public blog reads published rows and nothing else.

Idea→ Drafted→ Approved→ Scheduled→ Published

How a post gets made

  • The writing assistant reads a week or a month of journal days for raw material, plus the brand profile (voice, banned words, pillars).
  • It creates and edits drafts, generates a hero image and runs the checks. Every edit needs a reason and leaves an audit row.
  • I edit in the cockpit, approve, and pick a date. A daily cron publishes at 8am my time.
  • Publishing refreshes the post, the index, the home page, the sitemap and llms.txt, then fetches the live URL to confirm it responded.

The rules that keep it honest

  • Only I approve. The assistant has no approve, schedule or publish tool, and the database role it runs as can't touch those columns.
  • Approval is tied to the exact words. Approval stores a hash of the version. Change a comma and it's unapproved.
  • Publish gate. 600+ words, a hero image, a title tag, a meta description, at least two internal links, no banned words.
  • Live posts are locked. To change words on a published post, unpublish, edit, approve again. The original date is kept.

The public side uses the low-privilege key. Row-level security lets anonymous readers see published posts, brands and pillars, and nothing from /life. Pages are static and refreshed hourly or on publish, so a database hiccup doesn't take the blog down.

Guardrails

Let the database say no

This holds my health data and my private writing, and AI assistants can reach it. The safety lives in Postgres, not in app code that might have a bug.

One owner

Supabase Auth with an allowlist of one email. Every table has row-level security so a broken page can't leak rows.

A role per assistant

Ask runs as a read-only role. The personal assistant can read journal, goals and trips, and only add to-dos and a couple of measurements. The blog assistant can only write drafts.

Secrets stay server-side

The powerful key is used only by ingest routes and scripts. Each source has its own ingest secret.

Private files

Documents and photos sit in a private bucket and open through short-lived signed links after an owner check.

Ask before guessing

Extraction below 0.8 confidence, or any conflict (two hotels on one night), goes to the review queue instead of the tables.

Invisible to search

/life is noindex and left out of the sitemap and robots file.

The process

  • Brief first. A short HTML product brief: the question, the areas, the loop, wireframes.
  • Then a dev plan in Markdown: schema, workflows, and twelve PR slices, each with a gate that had to be true to merge.
  • Claude Code did the building, one small branch and PR at a time. I reviewed, merged, and Vercel deployed on push.
  • Use it, then fix it. Most of the later PRs are tiny: a field that got cut off, a button in the wrong place, a better default.

The stack

  • Next.js App Router and Tailwind, inside the existing personal site at /life
  • Supabase: Postgres, Auth, Storage, row-level security, separate project from the site's leads database
  • Vercel hosting and cron jobs
  • Claude for extraction, the monthly review and Ask
  • MCP endpoints so outside AI assistants get scoped access
  • Google Apps Script for the Gmail sweep

Building your own

If I were starting over

  1. Write your one question first. Mine is about intention. Yours might be about energy, family, or money. It decides which areas you build first and which you ignore.
  2. Pick three or four areas, not ten. Make the tables generic enough for the rest, but only build screens for what you'll open every day.
  3. Choose one daily writing habit and move it in. The journal is what makes everything else meaningful, and it's what feeds the blog.
  4. Import history once, then cut the cord. Backfill from the old tools, strip the noise, and turn the sync off. Living with two sources of truth is worse than either.
  5. Put everything through one inbox. Keep the raw item, let the AI propose, and queue anything it isn't sure about. You'll trust the data because you saw the doubtful bits.
  6. Give every AI its own database role. Decide what it may read and write in SQL grants, not prompts. Log every call.
  7. Make the blog a view of your writing. Drafts can be assisted. Approval and publishing stay yours, tied to the exact version you read.
  8. Ship small and use it daily. A plan with gates, short PRs, deploy on push. The best features came from noticing what annoyed me that morning.
